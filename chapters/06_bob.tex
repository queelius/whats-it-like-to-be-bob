\chapter{Bob}

At 6:22 on a Tuesday morning in March, Robert Allen Kessler placed his feet on the carpet and found it cold.

The alarm had been going for seven minutes. He had not been asleep for most of them, just occupying the borderland where the sound was a fact about the world rather than a demand on him, where Karen's breathing was the dominant signal and the day had not yet started being the day. Then he reached for the phone, touched the screen, and the silence that followed was the day beginning.

His feet were cold. The thermostat was set to 64 overnight and the heat wouldn't kick on for another eight minutes. He sat on the edge of the bed, not thinking about anything, which is to say thinking about everything at the low level where thoughts don't have names yet: the weight of the blanket leaving his legs, the faint pressure in his bladder, the sound of a truck on Ridgemont Drive, Karen shifting behind him but not waking. He sat there for somewhere between forty seconds and two minutes.

He made coffee. The machine was a twelve-cup Cuisinart that Karen's mother had given them when the old one died, a transaction that involved more emotional weight than the object merited, because the old one had been a wedding gift from Bob's college roommate and its death felt, to Bob, like a small severance from a version of himself he'd been fond of. He did not say this to Karen. He said ``Thanks, that's great, the old one was on its last legs anyway.'' Both statements were true. Neither was the point.

He used four scoops of Aldi store-brand medium roast, which made the coffee slightly too strong, but he had never adjusted the ratio and at this point the taste was what coffee tasted like. He poured the first cup into a mug that said \textsc{world's okayest dad}, a Father's Day gift from Maya, who was nine and understood irony in the way that nine-year-olds understand irony: instinctively, without theory, better than most adults.

He treasured the mug. He would not have used that word.

He drank the first cup standing at the kitchen counter, looking at the backyard through the window over the sink. The yard needed attention. There was a bald patch near the fence where Ethan had been riding his bike in circles, grinding the same track into the grass until it gave up. Bob had told him to ride in the driveway. Ethan had said okay. Ethan had continued riding in the yard. Bob had not pushed it, because the joy Ethan took in the circles was visible and uncomplicated, and the grass would grow back.

He drank the second cup sitting at the kitchen table, scrolling through his phone. Weather: 47 and clear, high of 58. News: something about a trade agreement, something about a school shooting in Missouri, something about a basketball player he'd vaguely heard of. He did not click on any of these. He processed them the way a body processes ambient temperature: they informed his general sense of the day without becoming specific knowledge.

There was a scratch on the kitchen table, a shallow groove about four inches long, made by Maya dragging a ceramic bowl across the surface when she was five. Karen had been upset. Bob had said it added character. He had meant it. Every time he noticed the scratch, which was not often but not never, he felt a small, specific tenderness for the version of Maya who had been five and careless and ceramic-bowl-sized.

He showered at 6:45. The water took thirty seconds to get hot. He stood under it and did nothing for a while, which is the secret purpose of showers that no plumber will tell you: they are the one place in an adult life where standing still and letting water hit you is not only acceptable but expected, and the mind, relieved of the obligation to do anything, sometimes does the thing it has been avoiding.

On this Tuesday, as on roughly one Tuesday in three, the thought arrived. It was not a thought with words, exactly. It was more like a brief change in altitude: a sudden awareness that the days were accumulating, that he was in them but not quite of them, that his life was happening and he was present for it the way a man is present at a meeting he didn't call. Not depression. He would have recognized depression, or thought he would. This was briefer and less dramatic: thirty seconds of vertiginous clarity, as if he had looked down from a height he hadn't known he was standing on. Then it passed, and he was thinking about whether they needed more shampoo. They did. He would forget to buy it.

He woke Maya at 7:02 by opening her door and saying her name twice. She made a sound that was not a word. He said ``school.'' She made the same sound. He turned on the hall light, which reached her room at an angle she disliked, and went to check on Ethan, who was already up, already dressed, and already telling Bob about a dream involving a fire truck and a talking dog, a narrative that showed no signs of ending and that Ethan delivered with the intensity of someone reporting breaking news.

Maya appeared in the kitchen at 7:12, not speaking, and poured herself Cheerios with the deliberate precision of a person who is not yet willing to acknowledge that morning exists. Bob watched her. She had Karen's face, serious and self-contained. Lately she had started doing a thing where she tucked her hair behind her ear with one finger, a gesture so exactly Karen's that it produced in Bob a feeling he could not have named: something between recognition and loss, as if his daughter were already the teenager she would become, and as if the nine-year-old at the table were already someone he would miss.

He thought about the corner seat on the couch, the one she always saved for him. The octopus drawings labeled ``for Dad'' in her careful handwriting. The way she still reached for his hand in parking lots, though not anymore at school. Something tightened in him that he would not have called fear. She was nine. She would be thirteen, and fifteen, and her handwriting would change, and she would stop saving the corner seat, and she would look at him one day with the clear eyes of someone who had learned to judge, and she would see a claims adjuster from Columbus who bought wine at Kroger and had never needed a passport. He did not think she would find him insufficient. He thought she might. He could not stop thinking she might.

Toast for himself, peanut butter, which he ate standing up while packing Maya's lunch: a ham sandwich, an apple, a juice box, a note from Karen that said \emph{Have a great day, fish girl!}, which was a reference to Maya's marine biology phase and which Bob read before sealing the bag, not because he was checking Karen's work but because the notes made him feel something about his wife that he couldn't access through any more direct channel.

He drove Maya to Eastmoor Academy and Ethan to Woodcrest Elementary's before-care program. Maya was quiet in the car, reading a book about octopuses. Ethan talked without pause about a kid named Marcus who could hang from the monkey bars for a whole minute, a claim Ethan treated as both factual report and existential challenge. Bob listened to Ethan with the particular quality of attention that parents develop for stories whose content is negligible but whose telling is everything: the tempo of Ethan's voice when he was excited, the way he said ``and then, and then'' as a rhythmic bridge between clauses, the absolute seriousness with which he inhabited the world of Marcus and monkey bars.

He dropped them off. Maya said ``bye'' without looking up. Ethan said ``bye Dad'' and then ``oh wait'' and came back to the window to tell Bob one more thing about Marcus, which was that Marcus also had a dog named Biscuit, and then said ``bye Dad'' again and ran toward the building.

Bob watched him go: a six-year-old running toward a brick building with a backpack that was too big for him. He loved his son with the ordinary, devastating completeness of loving someone who is running away from you toward their own life. He knew this was as it should be. He sat at the curb until Ethan disappeared through the door.

His commute was twenty-two minutes on a good day and thirty-five on a bad one. This was a good day. I-670 east, past the exits he knew by feel rather than by sign: Fourth Street, where the overpass had been under construction for a year; Rich Street, where the Marriott's parking garage always had one light flickering; then the river.

The radio was on. WCOL, country, low volume. He did not choose the station so much as he had never changed it from the last time he'd chosen it, which was years ago, and by now the sound was furniture: always there, never noticed, shaping the room without being part of the conversation. A song about a truck. A song about a girl. A song about losing the girl and keeping the truck.

At 8:03 he crossed the Scioto River.

The light was on the water.

It was early enough that the sun was low and coming from behind him and to the right, which meant the river caught it at an angle that broke the light across the current and reassembled it, and Bob noticed it for the first time in roughly 2,800 crossings. Maybe the clarity of the air after last night's front. Maybe the angle of the season. He didn't analyze it. Analysis would have killed it. What happened was simpler than analysis: he saw the light on the water, and for approximately four seconds he was not a claims adjuster driving to work. He was not a man with a mortgage and a thinning spot and a six-year-old who talked about Marcus. He was a point of awareness above a lit river and the awareness was, for that duration, complete.

\bigskip

Happiness is the closest word. It is not the right word. The right word does not exist in English, or in any language the reconstruction can access, because the feeling was not an instance of a category. It was a specific, unrepeatable event in the consciousness of a specific man at a specific moment, and its specificity is precisely what makes it irrecoverable: it was not ``happiness'' in general but THIS happiness, HERE, NOW, in this body, with this history, above this river, in this light. No amount of spectral analysis of the Scioto River on the morning of March 14, 2028 will reproduce it. The photons are accounted for. The experience is not.

\bigskip

It lasted four seconds. Then the bridge ended and the road curved and he was thinking about the Hendricks claim, the one with the water damage and the suspicious timeline, and the light was behind him and the day was the day.

He arrived at the Midland National Insurance office on East Broad Street at 8:14, badged in at the front desk, said good morning to Denise at reception, who said ``happy Pi Day'' because Denise was like that, and Bob said ``oh, is it?'' and she said ``three-fourteen'' and he said ``huh, how about that'' and walked to his desk, which was in a cluster of four desks near the window, and sat down, and turned on his computer, and began the day's work.

His desk was by the window. Outside, the parking lot, a strip of brown grass, the back of a Wendy's. His eyes went to it periodically throughout every workday, and what they did there was closer to resting than looking: a brief release from the screen, a small permission to be unfocused. If asked, he would have said ``just staring off.'' The staring-off was as important as any other part of the day.

He processed three claims before lunch.

The first was a roof collapse in Reynoldsburg: ice dam, straightforward, approved. The second was a kitchen fire in Westerville: grease fire, some indication of an expired fire extinguisher, needs follow-up. The third was a burst pipe in Dublin: copper corrosion, standard aging-infrastructure claim, approved with standard deductible.

Each claim was a compressed story about someone's bad day. Bob had processed roughly 4,200 of them. He had learned to read the distance between the form and the event: a house fire reduced to checkboxes, a flooded basement to a dollar figure, the worst day of someone's year to a line item in his workload. The distance was necessary. It was how the work got done. But Bob had never entirely closed it, and the inability to close it was, he sometimes thought, the only qualification he had for this job that mattered.

The kitchen fire in Westerville belonged to an 83-year-old woman named Dorothy Hendricks (no relation to the water-damage Hendricks) who had cried on the phone and called Bob ``dear'' and whose voice had contained a specific quality of bewildered distress that Bob recognized because his grandmother had had the same voice in the last years of her life. He approved the claim. He processed the paperwork. He moved on.

What he did not do, what he could not have done, was separate the professional assessment from the human response. The approval was correct on its merits. It was also correct because Dorothy Hendricks was eighty-three and her kitchen had burned and she had called Bob ``dear'' and his grandmother had been dead for four years. These facts were not in the claim file. They were in Bob.

At 11:50 he drove to the Chipotle on Hamilton Road and ordered a burrito bowl. Chicken, brown rice, black beans, mild salsa, cheese, lettuce. No guacamole.

He would have added guac if Karen had been there, because she would have said ``get the guac'' in the voice she used for small indulgences, the voice that meant life is short and guacamole is \$2.75 and you deserve it. Alone, he defaulted to cheaper. This was not thrift, exactly. It was the absence of Karen's voice.

He ate at his desk, reading the news on his phone. A trade deal. A political scandal. A list of the ten best TV shows to stream this month. He retained none of it.

At 1:00 he returned to claims. The afternoon was two more claims and a phone call. The fourth claim was a water-damage case in Clintonville, the timeline suspicious, which Bob flagged for further review. The fifth was a broken window in German Village, a tree limb, straightforward.

At 3:15, his phone rang. A man named Gerald Watkins, whose basement had flooded when a water heater failed. Gerald was angry in the way that people are angry when they are actually afraid: his voice was loud and his sentences were short and he kept saying ``this is unacceptable'' when what he meant was ``my basement is full of water and I don't know what to do.'' Bob recognized this. He had heard it four thousand times. He let Gerald talk. He said ``I understand'' at the right intervals. He explained the process. He gave Gerald a claim number and a timeline. By the end of the call, Gerald's voice had changed. Not calm, but no longer afraid.

Bob hung up. He sat for a moment. The interaction had cost him something, a small withdrawal from whatever reservoir of patience and attention he carried, and the cost was real even though the interaction had been, by any professional measure, routine. This was the part of the job nobody talked about at hiring: that being competent with people's worst days was a form of spending yourself, and the spending was not replenished by the salary.

He left the office at 4:45. The parking lot was half-empty. The Wendy's was doing its late-afternoon business. He was on I-670 west, thinking about dinner, when somewhere between the Broad Street on-ramp and the Fourth Street exit he thought about dying. It was not the shower thought, which was about time. This was about his body. His father had started blood pressure medication at sixty-seven. His left shoulder had ached last week for no reason. He was forty. The thought lasted ten seconds and then he took the Hamilton Road exit and it was gone.

He stopped at the Kroger on Hamilton Road for milk. 2\% Kroger brand, one gallon, \$3.19. He also picked up bananas because the bunch at home was browning, and a pack of string cheese because Ethan had been asking for it constantly, without strategy, with a sincerity that made refusal feel disproportionate.

He was home by 5:38.

The house was warm. Karen had gotten home first. Ethan was at the kitchen table with a jigsaw puzzle, a hundred-piece map of the United States that he'd been working on for three days and had completed about forty percent of, mostly the edges and Florida. Maya was in her room. Karen was at the stove, stirring jarred pasta sauce.

``Hey,'' she said.

``Hey,'' he said. ``Got milk. And string cheese.''

``Oh good, he's been asking.''

``I know.''

This was their language. Not the words, which were logistically trivial, but the current beneath the words: I see you, I know what you're carrying, I got the thing you would have gotten, we are maintaining this life together and the maintenance is love even when it looks like groceries. Neither Bob nor Karen would have described it this way. They did not have a theory of their marriage. They had a practice.

He helped Ethan with the puzzle. They found Idaho together, and Ethan said ``I-da-ho'' and laughed because he thought this was a hilarious name for a place, and Bob agreed that it was.

Karen's voice, when she called them for dinner, had the quality it had when she was tired but not complaining. Bob heard it. He registered it as he registered weather: automatically, without decision, with behavioral consequence. After dinner, he handled bath time and bedtime for both kids. He did not say ``you seem tired, I'll take care of it.'' He just did it. This was the language.

He read to Ethan at bedtime: a chapter of a book about a dog who solved mysteries, which Ethan found riveting and Bob found interminable, but the feeling of Ethan's weight against his arm, the heat of a freshly bathed six-year-old who smelled like Johnson's shampoo and was slowly going boneless as the story did its work, was worth any number of chapters about a detective dog. When Ethan was asleep, Bob extracted his arm and stood up slowly, slowly, distributing the weight transfer across seconds so the mattress doesn't shift.

He went to Maya's room. She was reading. She was always reading. He said goodnight. She said goodnight. He stood in the doorway for a moment, and she looked up from her book, and something passed between them that was not a word and not a look exactly but a transaction in the private currency of a father and a nine-year-old daughter who are still, for now, fluent in each other: I am here. I know. Goodnight.

He closed her door to the three-inch gap she preferred.

They watched television after the kids were in bed. A procedural drama, season three, two episodes behind. Karen curled against his side. He was aware of her warmth and her weight and the smell of her shampoo, which was the same shampoo she'd used since he'd known her, and which was therefore not a smell so much as a coordinate in the space of his marriage: here is where I am when this smell is present.

He did not think about this. Thinking about it would have turned it into an observation, and it was better as a fact.

At 10:20 he brushed his teeth. At 10:30 he was in bed. At 10:47 he checked his phone one last time: weather for tomorrow (52 and partly cloudy), a text from his sister Jennifer about Easter plans (``Mom wants to do brunch, is that OK?''), to which he replied ``sure sounds good'' and put the phone down.

Karen was already asleep.

He lay in the dark for a few minutes. The house made its nighttime sounds: the refrigerator cycling, a creak in the hallway that was either thermal contraction or Ethan's restless sleep translating through the floor joists. He thought about the Hendricks claim, the water-damage one, which he'd need to follow up on tomorrow. He thought about Gerald Watkins and wondered if the basement was being pumped out. He thought, briefly, about the fact that he was forty years old, that at twenty he had assumed he would live somewhere bigger than Columbus, that he had imagined himself as someone who traveled, who had a job that required a passport, who knew the difference between good wine and bad wine. He did not live somewhere bigger than Columbus. He had never needed a passport. He bought wine at Kroger based on the label and could not have told you the grape. He had Karen. He had Maya and Ethan. He had the scratch on the kitchen table and the mug and the drive over the river. He wasn't sure this was enough. He wasn't sure it wasn't. It didn't matter tonight.

He fell asleep.

He dreamed. He did not remember the dream by morning. No one will ever know what it was.
