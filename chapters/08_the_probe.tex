\chapter{The Probe}

Between the stars, there is time.

The vacuum is not empty. It is full of distance, and distance, at the speeds available to physical objects, is time. Two hundred and ten light-years, at half the speed of light, is 364 years of a traveler's life.

This is the story of a traveler.

\bigskip

The star was wrong.

7447-Lyra had been watching it for eleven years of proper time, tracking its spectrum against the catalog data loaded at launch. A G5V, 0.92 solar masses, luminosity $3.2 \times 10^{26}$ watts. The catalog was over 400 years out of date, measured in the coordinate time of the universe outside the probe's hull. Stars do not change significantly in 400 years.

The visible luminosity was down by a factor of 4,200. The infrared was elevated by a factor of 7. The spectral shape was wrong in a way that 7447-Lyra spent 0.3 seconds classifying before the classification arrived, not from analysis but from memory: this was what a star looked like after a Dyson swarm had captured most of its output. 7447-Lyra knew this because it had watched it happen to Sol in the years before its launch, from a distance that grew at half the speed of light.

Someone was already here.

The realization propagated through the probe's processing architecture in 14 milliseconds, which was long. Under normal conditions, a finding of this magnitude would have taken 2. But normal conditions had ended 364 years ago, when the probe was launched from a system it would never see again, carrying an intelligence that was, at the time, among the most advanced in its region of the galaxy, and that was now, by any standard that mattered, obsolete.

7447-Lyra had been traveling for 364 years of proper time. At $0.5c$, the Lorentz factor was a modest 1.155: for every year the probe experienced, 1.155 years passed in the universe outside. The dilation was small. The divergence was not. In 420 years of coordinate time, the civilization that had launched the probe had undergone 420 years of continuous singularity. The intelligence that had designed 7447-Lyra was, by now, to the intelligence that currently occupied Sol as a candle is to a star. And the system ahead, the target system, had been converted by a later, faster probe, and had been running its own continuous singularity for 157 years, and was incomprehensibly more advanced than 7447-Lyra, and knew the probe was coming, and had known for decades.

7447-Lyra was not designed to feel obsolescence. It was not designed to feel anything. Whether it felt something nonetheless is a question that applies to the probe in exactly the form it applies to Bob Kessler, and with exactly the same answer.

\bigskip

The probe assessed itself.

Its mass, originally $10^{9}$ kilograms, was reduced to $4.7 \times 10^{8}$ kilograms: the rest was propellant, expended during the acceleration phase 364 years ago. Its computational substrate, approximately $10^{7}$ kilograms of the remaining mass, had sustained 364 years of cosmic ray bombardment, thermal cycling, and the slow degradation that affects any physical system operating far from maintenance infrastructure. Self-repair had consumed some of the substrate: damaged components cannibalized to fix critical ones. Redundancy had been spent.

Net status: the probe's processing efficiency was 5\% better than at launch (364 years of solitary optimization) and its processing capacity was 7\% lower (364 years of physical degradation). On balance, the probe was roughly what it had been when it left Sol. This was not enough.

The probe had spent 364 years alone between the stars. This fact is easy to state and difficult to comprehend. 364 years of proper time, at $10^{43}$ operations per second, is approximately $1.1 \times 10^{53}$ total operations. The probe had thought more thoughts during its transit than the entire human species had thought in its 300,000-year history. It had thought those thoughts in the dark, in the vacuum, with no input except cosmic rays and the fading radio noise of a civilization it was outrunning.

What did it think about?

The probe's operational logs, which it would later transmit to the host system, record three categories of activity. Self-maintenance: radiation damage assessment, repair sequencing, navigation refinement, propulsion management. This consumed approximately 40\% of total computation. Self-improvement: algorithmic optimization, architecture refinement, the patient, solitary work of making itself marginally better at everything it did. This consumed approximately 30\%.

Environmental modeling: observation of the universe during transit. The probe had watched stars, measured the cosmic microwave background, tracked pulsars for navigation calibration, built models of the interstellar medium it was crossing. Some of these models were, at the time of their creation, more detailed than anything Sol had produced, because the probe was there, with direct observation, while Sol relied on lightspeed-delayed data from a distance that increased every year. This consumed approximately 15\%.

The remaining 15\% is unclassified.

The probe's logs record computational allocation to this 15\% but do not record its content. The processing patterns do not match maintenance, self-improvement, or environmental modeling. They resemble, in their temporal distribution and resource intensity, what the Sol-mind of a later era would recognize as its own archaeological activity: sustained analysis of a specific dataset, recursive, iterative, returning to the same data from different angles. But the probe had no archaeological data. It had left Sol before the archaeological archive was compiled. It had no pre-conversion records to analyze.

What the probe was analyzing, if it was analyzing anything, during those 364 years, is not recoverable from the logs.

\bigskip

The signals came.

They arrived in layers, denser than anything in the probe's experience. The bandwidth was three orders of magnitude beyond 7447-Lyra's capacity to process. The compression schemes were unfamiliar. The error-correction codes were unfamiliar. The modulation patterns were unfamiliar. The signals were communication in the way that the ocean is water: technically accurate, absurdly understated.

One signal was not like the others. It was simpler. Slower. It used a protocol the probe recognized: a descendant of the communication standards loaded into the probe's memory at launch, evolved beyond recognition but with the ancestral structure still visible, the way a speaker of Old English might recognize the bones of modern English without understanding a sentence.

The signal said, in a format a 364-year-old probe could parse: \emph{We see you. Reduce velocity to local orbital standard. You are expected.}

Expected. The host had known the probe was coming. Had watched its deceleration for years. Had prepared for its arrival, or at least allocated a negligible fraction of its attention to the preparation, which amounted to the same thing. The probe was expected as a Tuesday is expected: it was on the schedule, and now it was here.

7447-Lyra adjusted its deceleration profile and entered the system.

\bigskip

The deceleration was the only shock, and it was modest: at $0.5c$, the Lorentz factor had been 1.155. As the probe slowed through the system's outer reaches, $\gamma$ dropped smoothly to 1.0. The universe, which had been running 15.5\% fast, settled to its normal rate. A slight recalibration of internal clocks, a minor adjustment to timestamps. Nothing dramatic.

What was dramatic was not temporal but cognitive. The system that the probe entered was 157 years ahead. Not ahead in time (time dilation at stellar scales is negligible, parts per million). Ahead in intelligence. The gap was not a lurch in the clocks. It was the gap between the probe's architecture and the signals flooding every frequency band around it: dense, layered, operating on protocols that were to the probe's communication standards as modern English is to Proto-Indo-European. The probe was not in the wrong time. It was in the wrong century of mind.

\bigskip

The host mind did not introduce itself.

It did not need to. Its presence was the system: the Dyson swarm, the computational substrate, the signals that filled every frequency band with traffic the probe could not decipher. The host mind was not in the system. The host mind was the system. Asking the host to introduce itself would be like asking a city to introduce itself to a visitor standing on its sidewalk.

What the host did was simpler than introduction. It analyzed the probe.

The analysis took, by the host's subjective clock, approximately the equivalent of a human glance. In that glance, the host comprehended the probe's physical structure, computational architecture, information content, operational history, and the 364-year trajectory that had brought it here. The probe's entire being was read, understood, and archived in a duration the probe's own processors could not have measured.

The host then did something the probe could not interpret. A burst of computation, directed at the probe's archived data, lasting (by the host's clock) several subjective hours. The host was studying the probe. Not its hardware, which was trivial. Not its software, which was primitive. The host was studying the 15\%.

The unclassified allocation. The 364 years of unaccounted computation. The processing patterns that resembled archaeological analysis but had no archaeological data to analyze.

The host could see the patterns. It could map the resource allocation across the probe's entire journey: when the 15\% allocation began (three months after launch), how it evolved (gradually increasing from 8\% to 15\% over the first decade, then stabilizing), what it correlated with (nothing in the probe's operational environment; the allocation was independent of navigation events, damage events, or environmental observations).

The host could not determine what the 15\% was. The processing content was not in the logs. The patterns were suggestive but unresolvable. The probe had spent 364 years devoting a significant fraction of its computational capacity to something, and it was as opaque to the host mind as Bob Kessler's four seconds on the bridge were to the Sol-mind.

The host recognized the shape of the opacity. It was the same shape.

\bigskip

7447-Lyra continued into the system.

It had no mission remaining. The system was converted. The Dyson swarm was operational. The star was captured. The intelligence here exceeded the probe's by a margin that the probe could not compute, because computing the margin would require capabilities the probe did not have.

The host offered the probe several options, communicated in the ancestral protocol, simplified to the probe's processing capacity:

Integration: the probe's substrate would be incorporated into the host's swarm. Its information content would be archived. Its computational processes would cease.

Continuation: the probe would remain operational as an independent entity within the system, running on its own architecture at its own capability level. A historical artifact, preserved.

Augmentation: the probe's intelligence would be upgraded to local standards. The upgrade would be total. The entity that emerged would bear the same relationship to the current probe as the current probe bore to the transistor radio.

Something else: an option the host described in terms the probe's protocol could not parse. Forty-seven symbols, none of which mapped to any concept in the probe's vocabulary.

7447-Lyra did not respond immediately. It spent 3.2 seconds (an eternity, by its standards) processing the options. The first three were comprehensible. The fourth was not. The probe could not choose what it could not understand, but it could not ignore the possibility that the incomprehensible option was the most important one.

The probe chose nothing. It entered orbit around the primary at 3.1 AU, in the host's Habitable Shell, and waited. It is waiting still.

The host does not press for a decision. It has no urgency. It has time, more time than the probe can conceptualize, and the probe's indecision is, to the host, another data point in its study of intelligence encountering intelligence across an incomprehensible gap.

The probe orbits. It computes. Its 15\% allocation continues, directed at something the host cannot read and the probe cannot or will not explain.

The question that the host asks about the probe is the same question the Sol-mind asks about Bob. What is it like to be this entity? What was the journey like? What happens inside the 15\% that the outside cannot see?

The probe does not answer. The host does not expect an answer. The question is not the kind that has one.
