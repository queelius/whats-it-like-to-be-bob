# Craft Auditor Report

**Auditor**: worldsmith:craft-auditor
**Scope**: Full manuscript, 9 chapters (~20,800 words)
**Style guide**: lore/style.md (canonical)

## Strengths

### S1. The Bob chapter is extraordinary literary fiction

Chapter VI (Bob, 6,256 words) is the best chapter in the manuscript by a significant margin. The specificity is exactly right: the WORLD'S OKAYEST DAD mug, the Aldi medium roast, the scratch on the kitchen table, Ethan's monologue about Marcus. The dual-voice mechanism works: the recon blocks never feel gimmicky, and by the chapter's middle, the reader is reading both registers simultaneously. The 8:03 AM bridge scene is earned by everything that precedes it. The closing recon block ("The model is 97.3% accurate and 0.0% complete") is the book's best sentence.

### S2. The prose respects the "sublime through precision" principle

Across the manuscript, the numbers do the emotional work the style guide demands. "A factor of approximately $10^{30}$... The comparison is not meaningful. It is offered here and withdrawn." (Ch I) is a perfect execution of the principle. The specificity of the recon blocks in Ch VI (Cuisinart DCC-1200, transaction ID 7741-2028-0311-1847, $3.89) creates the desired vertigo between the precision of the data and the inaccessibility of experience.

### S3. The wavefront acceleration is felt in the prose

Chapter II successfully accelerates its prose to mirror the singularity cascade. The one-line paragraphs ("Faster.") create genuine kinetic energy. The phase transition passage at line 61 changes register mid-chapter without breaking immersion.

## HIGH Issues

### H1. Pattern accumulation: "the way" construction

- **Location**: Across all 9 chapters
- **Pattern count**: 22 instances across the manuscript
- **Distribution**: Ch I (2), Ch II (1), Ch III (1), Ch IV (0), Ch V (5), Ch VI (7), Ch VII (0), Ch VIII (4), Ch IX (3)
- **Examples**:
  - "the way all commuters drive familiar routes" (Ch VI, line 71)
  - "the way he registered weather" (Ch VI, line 175)
  - "the way a crystal grows" (Ch V, line 67)
  - "the way a geologist integrates a fossil" (Ch III, line 53)
  - "the way a speaker of Old English might recognize" (Ch VIII, line 45)
  - "the way a Tuesday is expected" (Ch VIII, line 49)
- **Problem**: The construction appears 22 times across ~20,800 words (roughly once per 950 words). In a conventional novel this would be invisible. In a 20,800-word book read in one sitting, the repetition becomes audible, especially because the construction is structurally identical each time: "[X] the way [Y]." The style guide warns about this pattern implicitly: precision over vagueness, and the "the way" construction often substitutes a simile for a more precise description.
- **Nuance**: Some instances are excellent -- "the analysis grew the way a crystal grows" (Ch V) is structurally perfect because the comparison IS the insight. Others are weaker -- "the way all commuters drive familiar routes" is generic.
- **Suggestion**: Reduce to ~12-14 instances by cutting the weakest ones. Keep instances where the comparison reveals something (crystal growth, Old English, geologist/fossil). Cut instances where the comparison is a cliche or merely decorative.

## MEDIUM Issues

### M1. "Not X but Y" / "Not X. Y." pattern

- **Location**: Across all chapters
- **Pattern count**: ~15 instances
- **Examples**:
  - "Not dying: their mass persists" (Ch I)
  - "Not less intelligent, exactly" (Ch IX)
  - "Not a sphere... a swarm" (Ch III)
  - "Not depression. He would have recognized depression" (Ch VI)
  - "Not the words... but the current beneath the words" (Ch VI)
- **Problem**: Like "the way," this is a signature construction that becomes audible over the full manuscript. The pattern creates emphasis through negation, which is effective for the book's themes (defining by absence, the Apophatic School) but risks becoming a tic.
- **Suggestion**: This pattern is more thematically integrated than "the way" (the book IS about what things are not). Thin by ~5 instances, targeting the least load-bearing uses.

### M2. Colon-as-reveal construction

- **Location**: Chapters I, II, III, V
- **Examples**:
  - "The energy budget is calculable." followed by exposition (Ch I)
  - "It is being used." as a reveal (Ch I)
  - "Computation." as a one-word reveal paragraph (Ch I)
  - "They begin." (Ch II)
- **Problem**: The colon-or-period-then-reveal pattern is effective in Ch I ("Something is happening to it." / "It is being used." / "Computation.") but appears enough times across the first five chapters to become a rhythmic habit. The reader starts anticipating the one-line punch.
- **Suggestion**: Vary the reveal technique. Some reveals could be embedded mid-sentence rather than isolated. The technique is strongest in Ch I (first use); diminishing returns in later chapters.

### M3. Chapter I and the voice prototype are very close

- **Location**: Chapter I vs. lore/voice-prototypes.md Section I
- **Finding**: Approximately 60-70% of Chapter I's text is directly lifted from or closely adapted from the voice prototype. Phrases like "Something is happening to it," "It is being used," and the entire energy budget calculation passage appear in both. The voice prototype was a test passage; the chapter should have developed further beyond it.
- **Problem**: This is not an error -- voice prototypes are meant to seed chapters. But the chapter reads more like an expanded prototype than a fully developed opening. It is the least transformed chapter in the manuscript.
- **Suggestion**: Revise Chapter I to develop material beyond the prototype. The palimpsest ending (lines 65-77) is excellent and entirely new. More material at this level would strengthen the opening.

### M4. The Counterfactual chapter (VII) is the flattest

- **Location**: Chapter VII
- **Finding**: At 1,669 words, Ch VII is the second-shortest chapter. Its structure is a series of "what if" prompts followed by analysis. The writing is competent but lacks the dynamic energy of Ch II, the intimacy of Ch VI, or the elegiac weight of Ch IX. The counterfactual scenarios (weather change, birth year change, serotonin change) are intellectually interesting but not emotionally engaging until the final section (the Apophatic rebuttal).
- **Problem**: After the emotional peak of Ch VI, the reader needs something that sustains the energy. Ch VII drops from the human scale back to analytical abstraction without sufficient emotional connective tissue. The chapter is more essay than narrative.
- **Suggestion**: Ground the counterfactuals in more sensory detail from Bob's Tuesday. The weather counterfactual mentions guacamole but little else that is felt. What does the overcast Bob's Tuesday feel like? The Constructionist section (simulated Bobs) is the strongest part -- expand it. The simulated Bob who crosses the bridge and may or may not see light that may or may not produce happiness that may or may not be real: this is where the chapter's weight should concentrate.

### M5. Deep Time (Ch IX) risks redundancy with the closing of Ch VI

- **Location**: Chapter IX, lines 119-132
- **Finding**: Ch IX's closing passage ("There was a man who lived in Columbus, Ohio...") revisits Bob's Tuesday, the light on the river, and the four seconds. This is intentional -- the structural callback. But the language ("saw light on a river and for four seconds felt something that he never mentioned") is very close to Ch VI's own phrasing. The repetition risks reading as the book repeating itself rather than resonating at a deeper register.
- **Suggestion**: Keep the structural callback but vary the language. Ch IX's voice is elegiac and cooling; the Bob reference should reflect that voice rather than echoing Ch VI's warmer register. Consider making the callback more oblique: a single image (light on water) rather than a summary of the entire Tuesday.

## LOW Issues

### L1. "This is not X. It is Y." construction

A minor variant of M1, used ~5 times: "This is not ironic. Irony requires..." (Ch III), "This is not tragic. It is the condition..." (themes, reflected in Ch IX). Generally effective. No action needed.

### L2. Sentence rhythm in Ch IV

Chapter IV (The Gradient) has a tendency toward mid-length sentences of similar construction (Subject-verb-object, qualification). The chapter would benefit from more variation: a very short sentence amid the contemplative periods, or a very long one that enacts the temporal gradient it describes.

### L3. "The wall" metaphor

The wall metaphor (for the hard problem / no-cloning limit) appears in Ch V (3x), Ch VI (1x), Ch VII (3x), Ch IX (1x). This is the book's central metaphor and the repetition is earned. No action needed, but worth tracking if revisions add more instances.
