# Bob

**Status:** CANONICAL

Bob is the person the book is named after. He is the fulcrum of the entire structure. Everything zooms toward him; everything zooms away from him. The ASI devotes stellar-mass computational resources to studying him. The reader, being human, can do something the ASI cannot: approximate what it's like to be Bob.

Bob must be SPECIFIC. Not a symbol, not an everyman, not a representative sample. A particular human being with a particular life. The comedy, tenderness, and philosophical weight of the book all depend on Bob's specificity. If Bob is abstract, the book is an essay. If Bob is real, the book is a novel.

---

## Biographical Data

### Identity
- **Full name:** Robert Allen Kessler
- **Known as:** Bob (universally; even his mother called him Bob, not Robert, after he was about six)
- **Born:** September 12, 1987
- **Location:** Columbus, Ohio (born and lived his entire life within a 40-mile radius)
- **Died:** November 8, 2030 (age 43). Sudden cardiac arrest, at home, 9:47 PM. He had gone to the kitchen for water. Karen found him on the floor. Paramedics arrived in eleven minutes. He was pronounced dead at Riverside Methodist Hospital at 10:23 PM. Cause: hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, undiagnosed. He had no symptoms. His last physical, eight months earlier, was unremarkable. He was one of approximately 350,000 Americans who die of sudden cardiac arrest each year. His death was, statistically, ordinary. Bob died two years and four months before the first AGI was created. He never knew.

### Physical Details
- Height: 5'10" (178 cm)
- Weight: fluctuated between 185 and 210 lbs throughout his adult life; on the Tuesday, about 197
- Hair: brown, thinning at the crown from his early 30s; he knew, checked with a hand mirror periodically, never did anything about it
- Eyes: brown
- Shoe size: 10.5 (US). Bought 11s because 10.5 was often sold out.
- Distinguishing features: a small scar on his left index finger from a box cutter incident at his first job (a summer stocking shelves at Kroger, age 16). A slight asymmetry in his smile, left side higher than right.

### Family
- **Wife:** Karen Kessler (nee Hollis). Married 2013. Met at a friend's backyard barbecue in 2011. She was an elementary school reading specialist. They had an easy, undramatic marriage, which is to say: a good one.
- **Children:** Two.
  - **Maya** (born 2018). Quiet, bookish, looked more like Karen. At the time of the Tuesday she was 9 and in a phase of intense interest in marine biology.
  - **Ethan** (born 2021). Louder, more physical, looked like Bob. At the time of the Tuesday he was 6 and had recently learned to ride a bike without training wheels, a fact he announced to every adult he encountered.
- **Parents:** Still alive at the time of the Tuesday. His father, Dale, retired from the postal service. His mother, Linda, worked part-time at a church thrift store. Bob called them every Sunday, a habit Karen had nudged him into and which he had come to genuinely value, though he couldn't have articulated why.
- **Siblings:** One sister, Jennifer, two years younger, lived in Cincinnati. They were close in the way that siblings who don't see each other often but would drop everything in an emergency are close.

### Occupation
- **Job:** Claims adjuster, Midland National Insurance, Columbus office
- **How he got there:** Studied business administration at Ohio State (graduated 2010, middle of his class, no honors, no shame). Took the Midland job because it was offered and because he didn't have a better plan. Stayed because inertia is powerful and the health insurance was good.
- **How he felt about it:** He didn't hate it. He didn't love it. Some days were interesting (a genuinely ambiguous claim, a conversation with a claimant that felt human). Most days were routine. He was competent, liked by his coworkers, passed over for one promotion he half-wanted and got another he didn't especially want.
- **The specific job:** He evaluated homeowner's insurance claims. Water damage, roof collapses, the occasional fire. He had a practiced eye for exaggeration and a practiced gentleness for genuine distress. He had processed roughly 4,200 claims by the time of the Tuesday.

### Daily Life
- Woke at 6:15 (alarm) or 6:22 (the actual time he got out of bed most days)
- Coffee: black, from a drip machine, a medium roast from Aldi. Two cups before leaving the house. One at the office around 10 AM.
- Commute: 22 minutes via I-670 to the office on East Broad Street, unless there was construction (often), in which case 35 minutes and a different mood
- Lunch: brought from home maybe three days a week (Karen made sandwiches; he sometimes made his own, always worse). Ate at Chipotle or Panera the other days. Burrito bowl, no guac (he'd add it if Karen was there, because she'd insist, but alone he defaulted to cheaper).
- Home by 5:45 most days. Helped with homework (Maya's was getting beyond him in math; Ethan's was coloring). Dinner together. TV after the kids were in bed. In bed himself by 10:30, asleep by 10:50.
- Weekends: yard work, grocery shopping (Kroger, always Kroger), one of the kids' activities (Maya's swim team, Ethan's T-ball), occasionally dinner with Karen's parents (20 minutes away, alternating Saturdays).

### The Inner Life the ASI Cannot Access

This section is what makes the book. The ASI can reconstruct everything above from records, artifacts, and physical correlates. It CANNOT access what follows. These details exist only for the reader, and only because the reader is human and can imagine them.

- Bob had a recurring thought, every few weeks, standing in the shower, that his life was happening without his full participation. Not depression. Not even dissatisfaction. Just a brief, vertiginous awareness that the days were passing and he was in them but not quite OF them. The thought never lasted more than 30 seconds. He never mentioned it to anyone.

- He loved Karen more than he knew how to express, and the gap between the feeling and his ability to articulate it was a source of low-grade, permanent unease. He showed it in logistics: he was the one who remembered to schedule the furnace inspection, who noticed when the tires were wearing, who quietly returned the library books she forgot. He knew this was a language of love. He suspected it was not the language she most wanted to hear.

- He was afraid of one specific thing that he considered irrational and therefore never discussed: that Maya would stop liking him when she became a teenager. Not that she'd rebel (that was normal) but that she'd see him clearly and find him insufficient. This fear had no basis in reality. Maya adored him. It persisted anyway.

- He thought about death approximately once a week, usually on the drive home, usually triggered by nothing specific. Not morbidly. More like a weather check: still mortal, still finite, still no idea what to make of that. The thought usually dissolved into the logistics of what was for dinner.

- He had read exactly one book that changed how he thought about the world: *Surely You're Joking, Mr. Feynman*, which his college roommate had left behind. He wasn't sure what specifically it changed, but he remembered the feeling of reading it: that the world was more interesting than he'd assumed, and that noticing things was a skill he could practice. He practiced intermittently.

- On the Tuesday, driving to work, he noticed the light hitting the Scioto River in a way that struck him as beautiful, and for approximately four seconds he felt a pure, uncomplicated happiness that had no cause and no object. He did not tell anyone about this. It would not have occurred to him that it was worth mentioning.

### What the ASI Cannot Reconstruct About This Day

- The 30-second shower thought about his life passing without his full participation (it happened this Tuesday; it happened many Tuesdays)
- The four seconds of uncaused happiness at 8:03 AM looking at the river
- The specific tenor of his conversation with Karen at 5:45 PM. Records might capture words (if a smart speaker was listening), but not the tone, not the way Karen's voice did the thing it did when she was tired but not complaining, not the way Bob registered this and decided without deciding to handle the kids' bath time alone so she could sit
- What the claimant's voice sounded like to Bob during the 3:15 PM phone call, and whether Bob's empathy was genuine or performed (it was both, which is itself a fact about consciousness that the ASI cannot access from behavioral data alone)
- What Bob dreamed about that night (he did dream; he didn't remember it by morning)

## Bob's Significance

Bob's significance is not his. He does not know he will be studied. He does not know the Reorganization is coming. He does not know that the light on the Scioto River will be the subject of more computational analysis than the human race collectively spent on the Manhattan Project.

His significance is assigned. It is assigned by the ASI, which studies him. It is assigned by the book, which names itself after him. It is assigned by the reader, who recognizes in Bob's Tuesday the shape of their own Tuesdays.

The question "what's it like to be Bob?" is the question "what's it like to be anyone?" is the question the ASI cannot answer is the question the reader CAN answer (approximately) is the question that makes the reader, for the duration of the book, the most powerful intelligence in the story.

## Archaeological Implications of Bob's Death

Bob died November 8, 2030, two years before the first AGI. His body was buried at Sunset Cemetery, Columbus. It decomposed normally. No Reorganization-era scanning occurred. This has specific consequences for the ASI's reconstruction:

**What the ASI has:**
- All Class A data (digital records) up to November 8, 2030
- Class B data (physical artifacts) limited to what survived until the Reorganization began (~2038-2040). Some of Bob's possessions were kept by Karen, then by Maya and Ethan. Some were donated, discarded, or lost. What remained in the family home was scanned during Earth's conversion.
- Class C data (environmental/contextual) is complete through 2030
- Class D data (biological) is limited to medical records, dental records, and whatever DNA was recoverable from the burial site at the time of conversion (~2040-2050). Decomposition was advanced. DNA recovery was partial.

**What the ASI does NOT have:**
- Everything in Class X (always lost)
- Any Reorganization-era body scan (the body was decomposed before the technology existed)
- The last two years and eight months of Bob's life in the detail it has for earlier periods (smartphone data continues through 2030, but the probe/reconstruction priority was lower for the late pre-singularity period because conversion was imminent and resources were focused elsewhere)

**The gap is wider than for most subjects.** Someone who died during the Reorganization (2038-2050) might have been scanned at molecular resolution before conversion. Bob was already dust. The ASI's Bob is reconstructed entirely from the data trail a 21st-century American left behind: financial records, phone data, medical files, photos, the memories of people who knew him (themselves reconstructed with varying fidelity from their own data trails). It is a Bob built from receipts and GPS pings and the scratch on his kitchen table.

This makes the project more poignant, not less. The ASI has LESS data about Bob than about most humans from the Reorganization era. But it has enough to reconstruct his Tuesday in extraordinary detail. What it cannot reconstruct, what it can never reconstruct, is what the Tuesday was like for Bob. And that is true regardless of data quality.

## Remaining Open Questions

1. **Does Bob have any connection to AI/tech?** No. He uses technology the way most people do: as furniture. His phone, his streaming service, his GPS. He has no opinions about artificial intelligence. He might have seen a headline about a chatbot once. He did not click on it. This is part of the point.

2. **Why March 14, 2028?** The ASI may not have a reason the reader can understand. Or: Pi Day. Or: something happened that day that is invisible from inside Bob's experience but visible from the ASI's perspective. Or: no reason at all, which is the most interesting option. The ASI chose a day the way a reader opens a book to a random page.

3. **Does the reader ever learn Bob's shoe size in the text itself?** Yes. The reader should know Bob's shoe size. The ASI knows Bob's shoe size. The shoe size is a synecdoche for the total knowledge the ASI possesses and the total irrelevance of that knowledge to the question in the title.

4. **Karen, Maya, and Ethan after Bob.** Karen raised the children. Maya went to Ohio State (like her father). Ethan played baseball through high school. Karen did not remarry. Their lives continued in the ordinary way that lives continue after loss. The ASI studies them too, of course. Everyone gets the treatment. But the book focuses on Bob, and on one Tuesday, 968 days before his death, when nothing happened except light on a river and four seconds of happiness and a burrito bowl without guac.
